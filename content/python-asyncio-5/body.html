Introduction

Parts 1 to 4 were about starting work: overlapping waits, running calls together, capping them. Each of those tools makes it cheap to start more work. None of them stops work by itself. Comparison

Consider ordering a meal by phone, then calling back two minutes later to cancel. A good restaurant tells the kitchen at once: the cook stops, the half-chopped onions go to the next order, and the delivery rider is not sent. A bad one takes the cancellation at the till and tells nobody; the meal is cooked, packed and driven to an empty house.

  • The phone call to cancel = the shopper closing the connection.
  • Telling the kitchen = cancelling the request's Tasks.
  • The cook stopping at the next natural pause = a coroutine stopping at its next await.
  • The meal already in the oven = work in a thread or at a seller, which a cancellation cannot reach.

The comparison stops working here: a cook who is told to stop can stop mid-chop. A coroutine can only be stopped at an await, and the coroutine itself decides what to do when the request to stop arrives.

Definition

Cancellation is stopping work whose result is no longer wanted. In asyncio it is a request made to a Task with Task.cancel(), which the Task delivers into its coroutine as a CancelledError exception. A stop reason is why the work is no longer wanted: the deadline passed, the client left, a sibling call failed, or the process is shutting down.

Gateway v5 knows two stop reasons: its own deadline (Part 3) and a seller call that fails with a bug (the TaskGroup, Part 3). This page adds the third, the shopper leaving, and the hardest one of all: the work that must finish even when everything else stops.

Comparison

A manager emails a colleague: "please drop the report you are writing". Sending the email takes a second and succeeds at once. The colleague reads it when they next look up from the keyboard, and then decides what to do: save the draft and stop, or reply "almost done, finishing it".

  • Sending the email = task.cancel(), which returns True at once.
  • The colleague looking up = the coroutine reaching its next await.
  • Saving the draft and stopping = a finally block, then letting CancelledError out.
  • "Almost done, finishing it" = a coroutine that catches CancelledError and carries on.

The comparison stops working here: the colleague might ignore the email for an hour. A parked coroutine receives the request in the very next turn of the loop; the delay is only as long as the step that is running.

Definitions

A pending cancellation is a cancel request the Task has accepted but not yet delivered into its coroutine. Task.cancel() arranges for a CancelledError to be thrown into the wrapped coroutine on the next cycle of the event loop, and returns False if the Task is already done or cancelled, True otherwise.

The docs say that, unlike Future.cancel(), Task.cancel() does not guarantee that the Task will be cancelled, because the coroutine can suppress the exception; suppressing it completely is not common and is actively discouraged. The docs call a Task cancelled when cancellation was requested with cancel() and the wrapped coroutine propagated the CancelledError thrown into it. So "cancelled" is a state the coroutine reaches by letting the exception out, not a state cancel() sets.

Working: what cancel() does, in CPython 3.14's tasks.py

  1. Refuse if finished. A Task that is already done returns False and nothing changes.
  2. Count the request. In CPython 3.14's tasks.py, Task.cancel() adds one to the Task's count of cancellation requests, cancels the Future the Task is waiting on if there is one and that succeeds, and otherwise sets a flag so that its next step throws CancelledError in. The count is the subject of the bookkeeping parts later on this page.
  3. Return True. Nothing of the coroutine has run. The Task is not done, and cancelled() is still False.
  4. Deliver later. In a later turn of the loop the Task takes a step that throws CancelledError into the coroutine. The next part traces that step.

Why each step is needed

  • Step 1: a finished Task has a result or an exception already; cancelling it would erase an outcome someone may still read.
  • Step 2: counting, instead of a yes/no flag, lets several parties cancel the same Task and later take back only their own request.
  • Steps 3 and 4: the caller of cancel() is itself running on the loop's only thread. The coroutine it cancels cannot run until the caller gives the thread back.

Introduction

Part 2 traced a Task through two steps with a result: the coroutine parks on a Future, a timer fills the Future, the Task's wake-up is queued with call_soon, and the next step resumes the coroutine with send(). A cancel uses the same path, with one difference at each end: the Future is cancelled instead of filled, and the next step uses throw() instead of send(). This part traces it with the same printing Future Part 2 used.

Working

  1. Park. As in Part 2, the coroutine awaits a Future, the Future travels up to the Task, and the Task adds its wake-up to the Future as a done callback.
  2. Cancel the Future. The deadline timer calls task.cancel("deadline"), which cancels the Future the Task is parked on (last part, step 2).
  3. Queue the wake-up. In CPython 3.14's futures.py, cancelling a pending Future marks it cancelled and schedules each of its done callbacks with call_soon. The Task's wake-up is now in the ready queue.
  4. Turn the cancelled Future into an exception. In CPython 3.14's tasks.py, the Task's wake-up reads the Future's result; if that raises, as it does with CancelledError for a cancelled Future, the next step throws the exception into the coroutine with coro.throw().
  5. Clean up. The exception appears at the await line where the coroutine is parked. Its except and finally blocks run.
  6. Finish as cancelled. In CPython 3.14's tasks.py, a step that ends with CancelledError leaving the coroutine cancels the Task; a coroutine that returns while a cancel has been requested but not yet thrown in also leaves the Task cancelled, not finished with its result. The Task's own done callbacks, such as a TaskGroup's, are then scheduled like any Future's.

Why each step is needed

  • Steps 2 and 3: the Task does not need a second way to be woken. Cancelling the Future it waits on reuses the wake-up it already left there.
  • Step 4: the exception is raised by reading the Future's result, exactly as an error set on the Future would be, so both reach the coroutine the same way.
  • Step 5: throw() raises the exception at the line where the coroutine paused, which is where its try blocks are. Part 1's generators taught this with gen.throw().
  • Step 6: marking the Task cancelled only after the coroutine lets the exception out is what makes cancelled() honest.

Introduction

Every seller call since Part 2 closes its connection in a finally, and that finally awaits. Part 4's rule was "clean up in finally and let the exception travel". This part shows exactly what that clean-up gets: how long it may take, what can interrupt it, and why the rule says "let it travel" rather than "never catch it".

Definitions

Clean-up is the code that releases what a coroutine holds, such as connections, slots and locks, whichever way it ends. The docs recommend try/finally blocks to perform clean-up, and say that a CancelledError that is explicitly caught should generally be propagated when clean-up is complete. The exceptions reference says CancelledError can be caught to perform custom operations when Tasks are cancelled, that in almost all situations it must be re-raised, and that it is a subclass of BaseException rather than Exception since Python 3.8.

Working

  1. The exception arrives at the await, as traced in the last part. The cancel request has now been delivered.
  2. An except CancelledError block may run, to log the stop reason or record a metric, and then ends with a bare raise.
  3. The finally block runs. It may contain await: the Task keeps stepping normally, because the request was delivered once and nothing has asked again.
  4. A second cancel(), if one comes while the clean-up is waiting, is delivered at that clean-up's await, and the rest of the finally is skipped.
  5. The exception leaves the coroutine and the Task ends cancelled.

Why each step matters

  • Step 2: catching to log is fine; the bare raise is what keeps the deadline and the TaskGroup working (Part 4, trap 5).
  • Step 3: an awaiting clean-up is allowed and common. Its cost is time: whoever waits for this Task waits for the clean-up too.
  • Step 4: clean-up is not protected. Anything that must finish whatever happens needs a different tool, which rung 3 builds.

Wrong belief: "A cancelled coroutine cannot await anything; every await after the cancel raises CancelledError again."

Why it is wrong: a cancel request is delivered once. After that, the coroutine's awaits behave normally, as the 20 ms close in the program shows, until someone calls cancel() again. The opposite belief is just as wrong: clean-up is not shielded, and a second cancel cuts it short.

  • Clean-up goes in finally; an except CancelledError that logs must end with raise.
  • Clean-up may await; it is not re-cancelled unless cancel() is called again.
  • A second cancel interrupts the clean-up at its await; whoever waits for the Task also waits for its clean-up.

Comparison

A meeting room has a "please leave" counter on the door. Each person who needs the room presses it once. When one of them gets what they wanted, they press "undo" once. Whoever is inside checks the counter on their way out: if it is back to zero, the requests have all been dealt with; if it is still above zero, someone else is still waiting for the room.

  • Pressing the counter = cancel().
  • Undo = uncancel().
  • Reading the counter = cancelling().

The comparison stops working here: the counter does not record who pressed it. Each party must remember for itself what the count was before it pressed.

Definitions

  1. Task.cancelling() returns the number of pending cancellation requests to the Task: the number of cancel() calls less the number of uncancel() calls.
  2. Task.uncancel() decrements that count and returns the remaining number; cancelling() and uncancel() were added in Python 3.11, mainly for asyncio's own use, notably by TaskGroup, and are not expected in end-user code.
  3. Since Python 3.13, when uncancel() brings the count down to zero, a CancelledError that an earlier cancel() arranged but that has not yet been thrown into the Task is withdrawn.

The docs say that code which suppresses cancellation by catching CancelledError needs to call uncancel() to remove the cancellation state. Rung 4 shows what happens when it does not.

Working: the rule every user of the count follows

  1. Remember the count when you start the work you may cancel.
  2. Cancel when your reason to stop arrives. The count goes up by one.
  3. Take your request back with uncancel() once your cancellation has done its job, that is, once the CancelledError has reached your code.
  4. Compare. If the count is back to what you remembered, the cancellation was yours alone: handle it, for example by raising TimeoutError. If it is higher, someone else also asked: let CancelledError travel on.

Why each step is needed

  • Step 1: the Task may already carry requests from outside when you start; only the difference is yours.
  • Step 3: without taking your request back, the count stays raised after you have handled it, and the next party to compare will believe an outside request is still waiting.
  • Step 4: this comparison is the whole point of counting. It is how a deadline avoids reporting "timed out" for a request whose shopper has gone.

Introduction

Working: the timeout's three moments

  1. Enter. In CPython 3.14's timeouts.py, entering the block records the Task's cancelling() count and schedules a callback for the deadline (with call_soon if it has already passed) that calls cancel() on the Task.
  2. Fire. At the deadline the callback cancels the Task. From here the CancelledError travels as traced two parts back, through the body's finally blocks.
  3. Exit. On exit after the deadline fired, the timeout calls uncancel(); only if the count is then at or below the value it recorded and a CancelledError is leaving the block does it raise TimeoutError in its place. In every other case the exception leaving the block travels on unchanged.

Why each step is needed

  • Step 1: recording the count at entry is "remember the count" from the last part. A request that already carried a cancel from outside does not confuse the timeout.
  • Step 3: the comparison is what keeps a shopper's hang-up from being reported as a timeout, and what keeps a nested inner timeout from swallowing an outer one.

How TaskGroup uses the count: cancelling its own body, and only that

Last part: a timeout converts only its own cancel. Still unsolved: a TaskGroup also cancels the Task that runs its body when a child fails. How does it stop that cancellation from leaking into the code after the group, and what does it do when the shopper has hung up at the same time?

In gateway v6, seller-c sends a malformed price, its Task fails with ValueError, and the request's TaskGroup cancels its other seller calls and the request Task itself. The request handler catches the ExceptionGroup and answers "502: bad price data". That only works if the group's own cancel of the request Task stays inside the group.

Introduction

Part 3 showed the outside of this: on the first failure, a TaskGroup cancels the remaining Tasks and the Task running the async with body, then raises an ExceptionGroup. Part 3 also showed its inside, a set of Tasks, a done callback each and one Future. This part adds the count to that picture.

Definition

The parent task of a TaskGroup is the Task running its async with block. The docs say that the first time a task in the group fails with an exception other than CancelledError, the remaining tasks are cancelled, and if the body of the async with block is still active the task containing it is cancelled too; that CancelledError interrupts an await but does not bubble out of the async with statement.

Working

  1. A child fails. Its done callback, run through the loop as in Part 3, records the error, cancels the other children and, unless the group is already shutting down, cancels the parent task and notes that it did.
  2. The body is interrupted. The parent's CancelledError arrives at whatever the body is awaiting, as traced earlier on this page, and reaches the group's exit.
  3. The group waits until every child is done, including their clean-up.
  4. The group takes its request back. In CPython 3.14's taskgroups.py, a group that cancelled its parent calls uncancel() on it at exit, and propagates the CancelledError only if cancel requests remain after that and no child failed.
  5. The group raises its ExceptionGroup of the children's failures.

Why each step is needed

  • Step 1: noting that it cancelled the parent is the "remember" half of the rule; without it the group could not tell its request from the shopper's.
  • Step 4: without uncancel(), the count would stay at 1 after the group, and every timeout or group later in the same request would believe an outside cancel was still waiting.
  • Step 5: the handler sees one ExceptionGroup, not a CancelledError, so it can answer "502" as gateway v6 does.

The case the docs call out: a group cancelled from outside that must also raise

If the shopper hangs up while the group is still cleaning up after a child's failure, the group has both a CancelledError to pass on and an ExceptionGroup to raise, and a single raise can carry only one. Since Python 3.13, a task group that is cancelled from outside and must also raise an ExceptionGroup calls the parent task's cancel() method, so that a CancelledError is raised at the next await and the cancellation is not lost. Python 3.13's release notes say the same change made task groups preserve the cancellation count, and that before it, when two nested groups both had a child fail at the same time, the outer group could hang.

Simple cases like case 3 behave the same. Wrong belief: "When a TaskGroup fails, its parent task has been cancelled, so the code after the group will get CancelledError too."

Why it is wrong: the group cancels its parent only to interrupt the body, then takes that request back with uncancel() and raises the ExceptionGroup instead. Case 3 shows the count back at 0 after the group. CancelledError reaches the code after the group only if someone else also asked.

  • On a child's failure, the group cancels the other children and its parent task, and notes that it did.
  • At exit it calls uncancel() on the parent; CancelledError travels on only if other requests remain.
  • Since 3.13, a group cancelled from outside that must raise an ExceptionGroup re-cancels its parent, so the outside cancel is not lost.

A TaskGroup's own cancel of its parent is taken back at the group's exit, so only a cancel someone else made can leave the group as CancelledError.

Introduction

Gateway v6 is gateway v5 with three additions. A watcher Task reads the shopper's connection and raises an exception when it closes; the request runs in a TaskGroup next to that watcher, so the group's own cancellation, from the last part, stops the request. The deadline is passed down to seller-e's SDK. And every request writes an audit row through an owned Task behind asyncio.shield(), which the next parts explain.

Working

  1. Read the request line, then open a TaskGroup in serve().
  2. Start the watcher. watch_for_hang_up() awaits reader.read(1). A shopper sends nothing after its request, so this read returns only when the connection closes, and then the watcher raises ClientGone.
  3. Run the request in the group's body: await self.handle(...), which runs gateway v5's compare() with its own TaskGroup of seller calls.
  4. On a hang-up, the watcher's failure makes the outer group cancel its parent, which is the Task running serve(). The CancelledError arrives inside compare(), whose inner group cancels the five seller calls and waits for their clean-up.
  5. handle() catches the CancelledError only to write the audit row "hung up after 100 ms", then re-raises.
  6. The outer group takes its cancel back and raises ExceptionGroup([ClientGone]), which except* handles. The connection is closed in finally.
  7. When the answer comes first, the body cancels the watcher and writes the answer; the group then has nothing to report.

Why each step is needed

  • Step 2: a hang-up is only visible to code that reads the connection. The watcher is that code, and nothing else.
  • Step 4: putting the request in the same group as the watcher reuses the group's cancellation instead of a hand-made one. The docs say terminating a task group is not natively supported by the standard library, and show how to do it: add a task that raises an exception to the group, and ignore that exception outside it. The watcher is that task.
  • Step 5: the audit row is written while the request is being cancelled. A plain await there would be safe from this cancel, which has already been delivered, but not from a second one; the next parts show why the write goes through shield().
  • Step 6: because the group takes its own cancel back, the server Task ends normally and can close the connection cleanly. No timeout reports the hang-up: each seller call's deadline sees a cancel that is not its own and lets it through (the timeout part).

What a cancel cannot reach: threads, processes and the seller's own work

Last part: one cancel stops every seller call in gateway v6. Still unsolved: "every seller call" meant every coroutine. seller-e's call is a thread, and every seller's work happens on another machine. What does a cancel do to those?

Introduction

A cancellation is an exception thrown into a coroutine at an await. Anything that is not a coroutine on this loop has no await for it to land on: a thread blocked in a system call, a process running Python code, a seller working on a request. For each of these, the gateway can stop waiting, but the work itself stops only if it was told in advance when to give up.

Definition

Deadline propagation is passing the time at which a result stops being useful down to every piece of work that cannot be cancelled, as a timeout it enforces by itself.

Working: what happens to each piece at the deadline

  1. The asyncio side. seller-e's call awaits the Future that run_in_executor() returned. The deadline cancels it, and the gateway stops waiting at 250 ms.
  2. The executor side. In CPython 3.14, run_in_executor() returns an asyncio Future chained to the executor's own future, and cancelling the asyncio Future calls cancel() on the executor's future. A concurrent.futures call that is currently being executed cannot be cancelled: cancel() then returns False. A call still waiting in the pool's queue is cancelled and never runs; a running one carries on.
  3. The thread itself. It is blocked in the SDK's socket read, and only the socket can end that wait: socket.create_connection()'s timeout parameter sets the timeout on the socket before it connects; in timeout mode an operation that cannot complete within it raises socket.timeout, a deprecated alias of TimeoutError since Python 3.10.
  4. The seller. Part 3 measured it: a seller keeps working on a request whose caller has gone. Only the seller's own limits, or a deadline the protocol carries to it, can stop that work.

Why each piece needs its own answer

  • Step 2: a thread cannot be interrupted from Python, so the executor can only cancel calls that have not started.
  • Step 3: the SDK's 5-second timeout was chosen by its vendor for "do not hang forever", not for a 250 ms budget, which is Part 3's lesson about client libraries again.

Solved example: seller-e's pool at 10 times the load

Given: 2,000 requests per second, each calling seller-e, and a pool of 4 threads. Every seller-e call is cut by the deadline.

Step 1. Without propagation. Each started call holds its thread for 900 ms. The pool starts at most 4 calls per 0.9 s, about 4.4 per second; every other call waits in the queue and is dropped by its deadline. The pool is always full.

Step 2. With propagation. Each started call holds its thread for at most the time left in its budget, about 0.25 s. The pool now starts 4 calls per 0.25 s, 16 per second, and is still always full: seller-e never answers within 250 ms.

Result: propagation frees each thread 650 ms sooner, so the same pool runs about 3.6 times as many calls (16 against 4.4 per second) with no extra threads, but for a seller that cannot answer in time those calls are still wasted. Propagation removes waste; it does not make seller-e useful. That decision is Part 4's: cache seller-e's prices, or drop it from the request path.

socket.timeout. Older code catches socket.timeout. It is the same class as TimeoutError since Python 3.10, as the claim above says, so both except clauses catch it; new code should use TimeoutError.

A socket timeout limits each operation, not the whole call. The SDK connects, sends, then reads, and the timeout applies to each of those separately. Passing 0.25 s bounds the read that matters here, but a slow connect followed by a slow read could take up to twice as long. When a library offers a total deadline, pass that; when it offers only per-operation timeouts, pass the remaining budget and keep the pool small.

Wrong belief: "Cancelling the Task that awaits run_in_executor() stops the function running in the thread."

Why it is wrong: it stops the wait. A call that is already running in the thread cannot be cancelled; only a call still in the queue is dropped. The program shows the thread running to 900 ms after its Future was cancelled at 250 ms, unless the function itself was given a timeout.

  • A cancel lands only on a coroutine's await; threads, processes and sellers never see it.
  • Cancelling run_in_executor()'s Future drops a call still queued in a thread pool, but not a running one.
  • Pass the remaining budget down as the timeout of anything a cancel cannot reach: seller-e's thread then ends at 250 ms instead of 900 ms.

A cancel stops the gateway's wait, not the work behind it; work in threads, processes and other services stops early only if the deadline was passed down to it beforehand.

A courier hands a signed parcel to a reception desk. The person who ordered it then leaves the building. The desk still signs the delivery book and keeps the parcel: the order's owner leaving does not undo a delivery already in progress. But the desk itself closes at 6 pm, and then nothing is signed for anyone.

  • The person leaving = the handler being cancelled.
  • The desk finishing the delivery = the shielded write going on.
  • The desk closing at 6 pm = the process shutting down (next part).

Definition

asyncio.shield() protects an awaitable from being cancelled by the coroutine that awaits it. If aw is a coroutine, shield() schedules it as a Task; if the coroutine containing the await is cancelled, the Task running inside shield() is not cancelled, but the await expression in the caller still raises CancelledError. The docs say to save a reference to tasks passed to shield(), because the event loop keeps only weak references and a task that isn't referenced elsewhere may be garbage collected before it is done.

Working: gateway v6's _audit()

  1. Start the write as an owned Task: task = spawn(self.audit.write(row), name="audit"). Part 4's spawn() keeps a strong reference in a set and reads the Task's outcome in a done callback, logging and counting any failure.
  2. Wait for it through a shield: await asyncio.shield(task). If nothing cancels the handler, this returns when the row is committed, and the answer is sent after the audit row exists.
  3. If the handler is cancelled now, its await raises CancelledError at once. The write Task is not cancelled; it goes on to its commit.
  4. The handler's CancelledError travels on, through serve()'s TaskGroup, as in gateway v6's part.

Why each step is needed

  • Step 1: a Task created inside shield() from a coroutine has no owner. If the handler is cancelled, nobody holds it and nobody reads its failure; spawn() fixes both.
  • Step 2: waiting, rather than firing and forgetting, keeps the rule "no answer without its audit row".
  • Step 3: this is the only thing shield does. It changes nothing for the caller and everything for the inner Task.

Given: gateway v6 at 2,000 requests per second; the audit write takes 20 ms; 1 shopper in 10 hangs up at a random moment during the 270 ms a request takes.

Probing question: "A request handler writes an audit record before responding. Clients sometimes disconnect mid-request. How do you guarantee the record?"

What a strong senior answer contains: the write must not share the request's cancellation: run it as its own owned Task and await it through asyncio.shield(), so a disconnect cancels the handler's wait but not the write. Then the limits, without being asked: shield does not survive a process shutdown or crash, does not protect a Task that the request's own TaskGroup owns, and does not read a failure that happens after the caller left; so the Task needs an owner that logs its failures, shutdown must wait for such Tasks, and anything that must survive a crash goes to a durable outbox written in the same transaction as the business data.

Wrong belief: "await asyncio.shield(task) makes the handler ignore the cancellation and wait for the write."

Why it is wrong: the handler is cancelled as usual: its await raises CancelledError immediately, as run 2 prints at 10 ms. Only the inner Task is protected. A handler that must still wait would have to catch the CancelledError around the shield, which is refusing a cancel, rung 4's first trap.

  • shield() protects the inner Task from the caller's cancellation; the caller still gets CancelledError at once.
  • Start the protected work with spawn(): a strong reference and a done callback that reads its failure.
  • A 20 ms unprotected write at 2,000 requests per second with 10% hang-ups loses about 15 rows a second.

What shield() does not protect

Last part: an owned Task behind shield() survives its handler's cancellation. Still unsolved: what else can stop that Task, and who hears about it when it fails?

A team wraps every "must finish" write in asyncio.shield() and stops worrying. Then audit rows go missing on every deploy; a refactor that moved the write into the request's TaskGroup loses rows on every hang-up; and when the audit store is down, the failures appear in no dashboard.

Introduction

shield() blocks exactly one path: the caller's cancellation travelling into the inner Task. Every other way a Task can be stopped or lost is untouched. This part measures four of them.

Working: four ways shielded work is still lost

  1. Its own owner cancels it. If the inner Task belongs to a TaskGroup, the group cancels it directly when the group aborts: the shield is not in that path. The docs say that if the shielded work is cancelled by other means, from within itself, that also cancels shield(). A TaskGroup cancelling its own child is another such means, as case 1 below measures.
  2. The caller stops waiting. A timeout around await shield(task) cancels the caller, which gets TimeoutError; the work goes on with nobody waiting for its result.
  3. The process shuts down. In CPython 3.14's runners.py, when the main coroutine finishes, asyncio.run cancels every task that is still running and runs the loop until they have all finished. A shielded Task is still running, so it is cancelled like any other. A crash or a kill stops it with no clean-up at all.
  4. It fails after the caller has gone. In CPython 3.14.0 to 3.14.8's tasks.py, once the awaiting caller has been cancelled, shield() no longer passes the inner task's outcome on; if the task later fails, shield() reports "<exception> exception in shielded future" to the loop's exception handler. The 3.14 branch after 3.14.8 no longer has that report: shield() stops passing the outcome on and reports nothing. Either way, no code of yours sees the failure unless the Task has an owner that reads it.

Refusing a cancel, and the bookkeeping you break

Last part: shield() protects work from its caller only. Still unsolved: the opposite mistake. What happens when a coroutine decides, by itself, that a cancel does not apply to it?

A price cache is slow today: 50 ms. Someone gives it a 20 ms limit by hand: a timer that calls cancel() on the current Task, and an except CancelledError: return None that falls back to the sellers. It works in every test. In production, shoppers who hang up during that lookup are never noticed, and some requests later end with nothing but CancelledError.

Introduction

Part 4's trap 5 caught CancelledError by accident, with a broad except. This part looks at code that catches it on purpose, to implement its own small timeout. It is the most common reason people reach for uncancel(), and it fails in two ways: it cannot tell its own cancel from anyone else's, and, without uncancel(), it leaves the count raised for code that runs later in the same Task.

Definition

To refuse a cancel is to catch CancelledError and carry on instead of letting it leave the coroutine. The docs warn that TaskGroup and asyncio.timeout() are implemented using cancellation internally and might misbehave if a coroutine swallows CancelledError, and that user code should not generally call uncancel().

Working: what a refusal changes

  1. The cancel is delivered and caught. The coroutine continues; the Task will not end cancelled.
  2. The count stays raised. cancelling() is 1 for the rest of the Task's life unless someone calls uncancel().
  3. The catch cannot see who asked. A cancel from the hand-made timer and a cancel from the shopper's hang-up look identical at the except.
  4. With uncancel() the count is right again, but step 3 is unchanged: the shopper's cancel is still refused, and now erased from the count as well.

Program

Three versions of the 20 ms cache limit, each in a request handler that then asks the sellers. First nobody else cancels; then the shopper hangs up 10 ms into the lookup.

Explanation of the program

  1. With nobody else cancelling, all three skip the cache at 20 ms and ask the sellers. Only the count differs: the hand-made version without uncancel() leaves it at 1.
  2. When the shopper hangs up at 10 ms, both hand-made versions catch the shopper's cancel as if it were their own timer's, skip the cache and go on to ask the sellers for nobody. The Task ends normally, cancelled=False.
  3. In asyncio.timeout(0.02) the hang-up's cancel arrives before the timeout's own timer has fired, so at its exit the timeout has nothing to convert, and the hang-up stops the request: cancelled=True. It needs no uncancel() in user code, because it does that bookkeeping itself.

Probing question: "When is it acceptable to catch CancelledError and not re-raise it?"

What a strong senior answer contains: almost never in application code. Catching it to log or clean up is fine if the handler ends with raise. The candidate mentions that the consequences differ between 3.11/3.12 and 3.13+.

Wrong belief: "Calling uncancel() after catching the CancelledError makes refusing safe."

Why it is wrong: it makes the count right, nothing more. The program's second version keeps the count at 0 and still swallows the shopper's hang-up, because at the except it cannot tell whose cancel it caught. Only a tool that remembers the count before it cancels, as asyncio.timeout does, can tell.

  • Refusing a cancel catches every cancel, not just yours: hang-ups and shutdowns are swallowed with it.
  • Without uncancel() the count stays at 1 for the rest of the Task.
  • For "give up on this step", use asyncio.timeout() and catch TimeoutError outside it.

A hand-made timeout that refuses its cancel also refuses everyone else's, and without uncancel() it leaves a false record that a cancel is still pending.

When it leaves the block, the timeout's timer has not fired, so the timeout cancels its timer, calls no uncancel(), and has nothing to convert: the CancelledError leaves the block unchanged, the except TimeoutError does not match, and the handler's Task ends cancelled at 10 ms.

A stale cancel: the request that fails with only CancelledError

Last part: refusing a cancel without uncancel() leaves the count at 1. Still unsolved: the count is only a number. When does a wrong number hurt, and how does it show up?

On Python 3.14, about one request in a thousand fails with no error in the logs except CancelledError. No shopper hung up and no deadline fired. The requests have two things in common: the price cache was slow for them, and a seller sent malformed data. On a test machine with Python 3.12 the same requests return a clean "502".

Introduction

A refused cancel is not a one-time mistake. The count it leaves behind is read again by every timeout and every TaskGroup that runs later in the same Task, and each of them assumes that a raised count means somebody outside is still waiting for the Task to stop. This part shows the TaskGroup acting on that false record.

Definition

A stale cancel request is a request that is still on a Task's count after the reason for it is gone, usually because a coroutine refused a cancel without calling uncancel(). Later code cannot tell it from a live request.

Working: how a stale request ends a later request on 3.13 and 3.14

  1. The hand-made cache limit refuses its own cancel at 20 ms. Count: 1.
  2. The handler calls compare(). seller-c answers seller-c 12x9; parsing the price raises ValueError, a bug, so it fails the inner TaskGroup.
  3. The group cancels its parent, the handler's Task, to interrupt its body. Count: 2.
  4. At exit the group takes its request back. Count: 1. A request remains, so the group believes an outside cancel is pending.
  5. The group must raise its ExceptionGroup, so, as the TaskGroup part showed, it re-cancels its parent to keep that "outside" cancel from being lost.
  6. The handler's except* ValueError sets the reply "502". The re-arranged CancelledError arrives at its next await, the audit write, and the request ends cancelled: no reply, no audit row.

Clean-up that awaits too long: the deadline fires on time, the answer is late

Last part: a stale request ends a later request. Still unsolved: rung 2 said clean-up may await and that whoever waits for the Task waits for its clean-up too. What does that cost when the clean-up is slow?

A seller asks its callers to tell it when they give up, so it can stop working on the request. Someone adds that call to seller-e's clean-up: on CancelledError, send "cancel my request" to seller-e, then re-raise. seller-e takes 400 ms to answer that call too. From then on every request answers at 650 ms, though the deadline still fires at 250 ms and seller-e is still counted as timed out.

Introduction

Every tool on the request path waits for its Tasks to finish, clean-up included: a timeout's block cannot exit before the CancelledError leaves it, and a TaskGroup cannot exit before every child is done. So clean-up time is added, in full, to the time the request takes after its deadline. This part measures it and bounds it.

Working

  1. At 250 ms seller-e's deadline fires and CancelledError arrives at its readline().
  2. The except awaits the 400 ms notice. The cancel was delivered once; nothing interrupts the notice.
  3. At 650 ms the notice ends, the CancelledError leaves the timeout's block, and the timeout turns it into TimeoutError: seller-e is counted as timed out, correctly.
  4. Only now is seller-e's Task done, and the TaskGroup, which waits for all five, lets the request answer.

Program

Explanation of the program

  2. Unbounded, the answer is right (seller-b at 1,199, seller-e timed out) and arrives at 650 ms: 250 ms of deadline plus 400 ms of clean-up.
  3. Bounded to 20 ms with its own asyncio.timeout, the clean-up gives up on the notice and the answer arrives at 270 ms.   • Timeouts and TaskGroups wait for clean-up; slow clean-up is added in full after the deadline.
  • Signal: latency above the budget with timeouts counted; cancellation latency well above zero.
  • Fix: bound clean-up with its own timeout, move slow parts to an owned background Task, keep clean-up local.

A deadline bounds the wait, not the clean-up after it, so every await in clean-up must be short, bounded, or moved off the request's path.

Hang-ups at 10 times the load, and a client that hangs up on purpose

Last part: slow clean-up makes a timely deadline produce a late answer. Still unsolved: what all of this does at 2,000 requests per second, when hang-ups come in bursts, or from someone doing it on purpose.

A sale page refreshes itself every few seconds, and each refresh abandons the previous request. A misbehaving client opens a connection, sends a request line and closes it, a thousand times a second. Gateway v5 does the full fan-out for every one of them; gateway v6 must not.

Introduction

Rung 1 measured one hang-up. This part measures a burst on both gateways, then does the arithmetic at 10 times the load, and covers the client that makes hang-ups its attack.

Program

Ten shoppers send their request and hang up 50 ms later. The program counts the seller calls still in progress 10 ms after the last hang-up, and the seller-e SDK threads still busy at 300 ms.

Explanation of the program

  1. Gateway v5 behind a plain server: 50 seller calls are still in progress at 60 ms, all 5 calls of all 10 abandoned requests, and seller-e's 4 SDK threads are still busy at 300 ms. They stay busy until about 900 ms.
  2. Gateway v6: 0 calls in progress at 60 ms and 0 threads busy at 300 ms. Each watcher cancelled its request; the 6 seller-e calls still queued in the pool were dropped, and the 4 that had started gave up at their passed-down deadlines.

Solved example: 10 times the load

Given: 2,000 requests per second; 10% of shoppers hang up, on average 100 ms in. Per abandoned request, gateway v5 keeps 0.32 connection-seconds of seller calls and 0.8 thread-seconds of seller-e's pool busy for nobody (rung 1). Gateway v6 stops within a loop turn or two of the hang-up.

Step 1. Abandoned requests: 2,000 × 0.1 = 200 per second.

Step 2. Gateway v5, connections. 200 × 0.32 = 64 seller connections open for nobody at any moment, each also a file descriptor (Part 2) and a slot under the shared cap (Part 3).

Step 3. Gateway v5, seller-e's pool. 200 × 0.8 = 160 thread-seconds wanted per second from 4 threads: the pool is full of abandoned calls, and live requests' seller-e calls wait in its queue.

Step 4. Gateway v6. Seller calls stop at the hang-up: about 0 connections for nobody. Thread time per abandoned call drops from 0.8 s to at most the 0.15 s left in its budget at 100 ms, because the deadline was passed down: 200 × 0.15 = 30 thread-seconds a second, still more than 4 threads can give, but no longer outlasting the request.

Result: what breaks first at 10 times the load on gateway v5 is seller-e's pool, then the shared cap. Gateway v6 removes the connection waste entirely and cuts the thread waste by more than 80%; what remains is Part 4's lesson that seller-e does not belong on the request path.

The client that hangs up on purpose

A client that opens a connection, sends a request and closes it at once costs it almost nothing and costs gateway v5 a full fan-out: five seller calls, a thread for 900 ms, ranking and counts. At 1,000 such requests per second that is 1,000 × 0.8 = 800 thread-seconds of seller-e's time wanted each second. On gateway v6 the request is cancelled as soon as the watcher reads the end of the connection, so the attacker has to keep each connection open for as long as it wants the gateway to work, which is the point aiohttp's documentation makes for its own handler cancellation (in gateway v6's part). Rate limits per client, in front of the gateway, are still needed; cancellation only removes the leverage.

Work that must finish: shield, an owned Task, or a durable outbox

Last part: cancellation, propagated, keeps the gateway's resources for live shoppers. Still unsolved: for the work that must not be cancelled, which of the three tools on this page should a team pick?

Three teams each have one piece of must-finish work. The gateway team has its audit row. The checkout team has an order confirmation e-mail. The analytics team has the per-seller counts. All three say "use shield()". At most one of them is right.

Introduction

Every tool for must-finish work answers two questions differently: does the request wait for the work, and what can still destroy the work? The comparison below puts the three options from this page, plus the plain await they replace, against the threats the last rungs measured.

Definitions

  1. An owned Task is a background Task started with Part 4's spawn(): a strong reference in a set, and a done callback that reads, logs and counts its failure. The request does not wait for it.
  2. A durable outbox is a table written in the same database transaction as the change it belongs to, listing work still to be done; a separate worker reads it and does that work, retrying until it succeeds. A durable queue plays the same role when the work starts outside a database.

Decision rules

  • Ask first whether the work may be lost. If losing it on a crash is unacceptable, no in-process tool is enough: write it durably, in the same transaction as the change it belongs to.
  • If it may be lost on a crash, ask whether the answer must wait for it. If yes: owned Task behind shield(). If no: owned Task, not awaited.
  • Never shield a bare coroutine, or a Task the request's own TaskGroup owns.
  • Every in-process option needs shutdown to wait for owned Tasks before the loop closes; Part 8 builds that.

Solved example: the three teams

Given: the three pieces of work from the problem.

Step 1. Audit row. The rule is "no answer without its row", so the answer waits; a row lost in a crash is a gap the audit team accepts, because the crash itself is logged. Owned Task behind shield(), as gateway v6 does.

Step 2. Order confirmation. It must be sent exactly once per order whatever happens, and the shopper should not wait for an e-mail server. An outbox row written with the order, and a worker that sends and retries; the e-mail service must tolerate a duplicate after a retry.

Step 3. Per-seller counts. Losing a second of counts on a crash is harmless, and no request should wait. Owned Task, not awaited, as gateway v5's flusher.

Result: one team should use shield(), one an owned background Task, and one an outbox. "Use shield()" was right for the audit row only.

Probing question: "Why not use a durable outbox for everything that must finish?"

What a strong senior answer contains: because it costs a table or broker, a worker, retries and duplicate handling, and moves the work out of the request: the answer can no longer say the work is done. Use it where a lost or doubled action costs money or trust (a payment, an e-mail, an order event). For records whose loss on a crash is acceptable, an owned Task is simpler, and shield() adds "the answer waits for it". The candidate states the guarantee each gives: shield and owned Tasks survive cancellation, not crashes; an outbox survives crashes, at the price of at-least-once delivery.

Wrong belief: "shield() is the asyncio way to make work reliable."

Why it is wrong: shield() makes work independent of one caller's cancellation. Reliability against shutdown needs an owner that shutdown waits for, and reliability against a crash needs the work written somewhere durable before it is promised. The table's last three columns are where shield() and an outbox differ.

  • Must survive a crash: durable outbox or queue, at-least-once, with duplicates handled.
  • May be lost on a crash, answer waits: owned Task behind shield(); answer does not wait: owned Task.
  • Every in-process option needs a shutdown that waits for owned Tasks.

Pick by what may destroy the work: shield() handles a caller's cancel, an owner and a waiting shutdown handle the rest of the process, and only a durable outbox handles a crash.

A payment service must call the bank's API exactly once per payment and confirm to the client only after the bank has accepted it. Requests are sometimes cancelled by client disconnects and the service is redeployed daily. Which design fits?

  1. An owned Task behind shield(), awaited before confirming
  2. Write a payment row and an outbox row in one transaction, let a worker call the bank with an idempotency key and retries, and confirm when the row says accepted
  3. An owned Task, not awaited, that calls the bank

A payment must survive crashes and deploys and must not happen twice. Only a durable record written with the payment survives a crash, and an idempotency key makes the worker's retries safe. The client can wait on the row's status instead of on a coroutine.

Interview drill: stopping work in an async service

Last part: pick a must-finish tool by what may destroy the work. Still unsolved: explaining cancellation out loud, with the mechanism, the numbers and the trade-offs, the way an interviewer asks.

"What happens when you cancel an asyncio Task?" Most answers say "it raises CancelledError". A senior answer says where, when, who decides, how the deadline and the TaskGroup know it was theirs, and what cannot be reached at all.

Introduction

Three prompts follow. Each draws on this page and at least one earlier part. Try each before opening the reference answer.

Prompt 1: "Walk me through task.cancel()"

seller-e's Task is parked in await reader.readline() inside asyncio.timeout_at(deadline). The deadline passes. Walk through everything from the timer to the gateway recording "seller-e timed out".

The timeout's timer callback runs in the loop and calls task.cancel(). That counts the request (cancelling goes from 0 to 1), cancels the waiter Future the Task is parked on and returns True; nothing of the coroutine has run. Cancelling the Future schedules the Task's wake-up with call_soon. In the next iteration the wake-up reads the Future's result, which raises CancelledError, and the Task's step throws it into the coroutine at the readline() await. The coroutine's finally closes the connection; the exception leaves the coroutine and reaches the timeout's exit, which calls uncancel(), finds the count back at the 0 it recorded at entry, and raises TimeoutError instead. The seller call catches TimeoutError and returns it as a value, and the gateway records a timeout. Trade-off worth naming: the gateway stopped waiting, but seller-e's own work, and any thread behind the call, carry on unless the deadline was passed down to them.

Prompt 2: "Design for disconnects and must-finish work"

A ticket-booking API fans out to four suppliers per search and writes a ledger row for every booking. Clients disconnect often, and the service is deployed hourly. Design how searches stop and how ledger rows survive, and say what each choice gives up.

Each request runs in a TaskGroup next to a watcher (or the framework's handler cancellation), so a disconnect cancels the request's Task and the group carries that cancel to all four supplier calls, each stopping at its await and closing its connection in finally. Every call that a cancel cannot reach, such as a blocking SDK in a thread, gets the remaining budget as its own timeout. The ledger write is started as an owned Task, outside the request's group, and awaited through shield(), so a disconnect cancels the handler's wait but not the write; that costs the write's time on the answer path. Deploys: shutdown first waits for the owned Tasks, with a limit, so hourly deploys do not cut writes. If a booking must never be lost even on a crash, the ledger row is written in the booking's own transaction, or as an outbox row a worker processes, which gives up "the answer means it is written" for at-least-once delivery.

Prompt 3: "After upgrading to Python 3.13, some requests fail with only CancelledError"

After an upgrade from 3.12 to 3.13, about one request in a thousand ends with only CancelledError. Nothing hung up and no timeout fired. What do you suspect, how do you confirm it, and how do you fix it?

A refused cancel earlier in the same Task: some code catches CancelledError and continues without uncancel(), often a hand-made timeout around a cache or a retry. That leaves a stale request on the count. Since 3.13, a TaskGroup that later fails in the same Task and still sees requests pending re-cancels its parent so as not to lose them, so the next await after the handled ExceptionGroup raises CancelledError. On 3.12 the group never re-cancelled, which is why the bug was invisible before. Confirm by logging cancelling() at the start of the fan-out (above 0 in a request nobody cancelled) and by searching for except CancelledError without raise. Fix by replacing hand-made limits with asyncio.timeout() and catching TimeoutError outside it. Trade-off: none worth keeping; the refusal also swallowed real hang-ups.

What separates a senior answer here: it treats cancellation as a request that travels (a count, a Future cancelled, a wake-up queued, an exception thrown at an await) rather than as a kill; it names the stop reasons and how each tool tells its own cancel from another's; it uses numbers (a hang-up at 100 ms costs 0.32 connection-seconds and 0.8 thread-seconds; clean-up turns 250 ms into 650 ms); and it states what each protection does not cover: shield against shutdown and crashes, cancel against threads and other services.

Wrong belief: "In an interview, 'cancel raises CancelledError inside the task' is a complete answer."

Why it is wrong: it skips everything that goes wrong in production: when the exception arrives (only at an await), who can refuse it (the coroutine), how a timeout or group knows it was theirs (the count), what is out of reach (threads, other services), and what must be protected from it (must-finish work). Interviewers probe exactly those gaps.

  • cancel() → count + cancelled Future → wake-up via call_soon → throw at the await → finally → done.
  • Disconnect → one cancel of the request Task → TaskGroup carries it; deadlines passed down; must-finish work protected deliberately.
  • "Only CancelledError" after 3.13: look for refused cancels and stale counts.

A senior answer about cancellation follows the request from cancel() to done, names who decides at each step, and says what no cancel can reach.
