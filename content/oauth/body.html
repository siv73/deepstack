Why the app shouldn't get your password

PrintNest, a photo-printing site, wants 40 photos from your photo cloud. The old way: you type your photo-cloud password into PrintNest.

Introduction

Many applications need data that is stored on some other service. A printing site needs your photos. A calendar app needs your contacts. An invoicing tool needs your bank statements. In each case one program (the app) must read data that belongs to you and lives on another program (the service).

The question this whole page answers is: how can the app get that data without becoming you? Let us first see what goes wrong with the simplest answer, which is to hand over your password.

The problem with sharing a password

Suppose you give your photo-cloud password to PrintNest. That one step goes wrong in three ways:

  1. Too much power. PrintNest can now do anything you can: read your email, delete every photo, change your password. You wanted to share 40 photos. You have shared your whole account.
  2. No clean way to take it back. To cut PrintNest off, you must change your password, which also breaks every other app that stored it.
  3. One more place to leak from. PrintNest now stores a password it can leak. If PrintNest's database is stolen, your photo-cloud account is stolen with it.

Solved example: counting the damage

Given: you use five apps that read your photo cloud: PrintNest, a collage maker, a backup tool, a frame shop and a slideshow app. All five store your password. You decide to stop using PrintNest.

Step 1. PrintNest knows your password. The only way to make that password useless to PrintNest is to change it.

Step 2. After the change, all five apps hold the old password. All five stop working, not only PrintNest.

Step 3. You must type the new password into the four apps you still want. Each of those four now stores the new password, so the risk is back where it started.

Result: stopping one app cost you five broken apps and four re-logins, and PrintNest could still have read everything until the moment you changed the password. We want a design where stopping PrintNest touches PrintNest only.

The idea: give a limited key, not the house keys

Think of a valet key for a car. It starts the car but won't open the boot, and you can cancel it without changing the locks. PrintNest needs a valet key, not your house keys.

The mapping is direct:

  • The car owner = you.
  • The valet = PrintNest.
  • The valet key = a limited key that allows only "read photos".
  • The car company that cuts the valet key = your photo cloud's own login page.

Where the comparison stops working: a valet key is a physical object, so copying it takes effort. The digital key in OAuth is a string of characters. Anyone who copies the string can use it. Part 2 returns to this weakness.

Working: how the limited key is handed over

  1. PrintNest sends you to your photo cloud's own login page.
  2. You log in there. PrintNest never sees the password.
  3. You approve one thing: "PrintNest may read photos".
  4. The photo cloud hands PrintNest a limited key.
  5. PrintNest uses the key. You can cancel it any time.

Why each step is needed.

  • Step 1 moves the login away from PrintNest. If PrintNest showed its own login form, you would be typing the password into PrintNest again.
  • Step 2 means the password is typed only into the service that already knows it. No new party learns it.
  • Step 3 lets you decide the limit. Without it, the service would not know what PrintNest is allowed to do.
  • Step 4 gives PrintNest something to show on every request. Without a key, the service could not tell PrintNest's requests from a stranger's.
  • Step 5 is the reason for the whole design. Each app has its own key, so cancelling one key stops one app.

Definition

OAuth 2.0 is a set of rules by which an app gets limited, cancellable access to a user's data on another service, with the user's approval, without the app ever learning the user's password.

This hand-over is OAuth 2.0. The spec names four roles: the resource owner (you), the client (the app), the authorization server (issues the keys) and the resource server (hosts the data). This page uses plain names: you, PrintNest, the login server and the Photos API.

Explanation of the diagram. The four boxes are the four roles. In step 1 the only arrow from you goes to the login server: your password travels to one place only. In step 2 the dashed arrow (a reply) carries the limited key from the login server to PrintNest. In step 3 PrintNest calls the Photos API and attaches the key. Notice that there is no arrow from you to PrintNest carrying a password. That missing arrow is the point of OAuth.

Advantages over sharing the password

  1. The app gets only what you approved, for example "read photos", not "delete photos".
  2. Each app has its own key, so you can stop one app without touching the others.
  3. The password stays with the one service that needed it, so a breach at PrintNest does not leak it.

Wrong belief: "OAuth is a way to log in", because most people first meet it as a "Sign in with Google" button.

Why it is wrong: OAuth answers a different question: what may this app do with your data? Knowing who you are is a separate job, added on top by another standard. Part 13 shows the difference and why mixing them up causes bugs.

  • Sharing a password gives full, permanent, shared access.
  • OAuth gives each app its own limited key, issued after you log in at the service itself.
  • Four roles: you, the app, the login server, the API.

OAuth lets an app act for you with a limited, cancellable key, and the app never sees your password.

You want to stop PrintNest but keep your other apps working. With OAuth, what do you do?

  1. Change your photo-cloud password
  2. Cancel PrintNest's key at your photo cloud
  3. Delete your PrintNest account

Each app gets its own key, so you can cancel one without touching the others.

In your own words: why is handing your password to a third-party app a bad design, and what does OAuth do instead?

A password gives the app everything you can do, forever, and the only way to take it back is to change the password for every app. OAuth sends you to the login server you already trust. You log in there and approve a narrow permission. The app gets a limited key that you can cancel on its own.

The key is a token, and whoever holds it can use it

PrintNest now sends 40 requests for 40 photos. The Photos API must decide, 40 times, whether each request is allowed.

Introduction

The API cannot ask you each time. You are not at the keyboard; PrintNest is running on its own servers. So every request must carry proof that you approved it. That proof is the "limited key" from Part 1. In this part we give it its proper name and see how it travels.

An everyday comparison

Think of a hotel key card. The front desk checks your ID once and programs a card for room 412 until Friday. The door never asks your name. It only checks the card.

  • The front desk = the login server (checks who you are, once).
  • The key card = the key PrintNest receives.
  • "Room 412 until Friday" = what the key allows and for how long.
  • The door lock = the Photos API (checks the card on every visit).

Definitions

The card is an access token. An access token is a string that represents an authorization issued to the client. What it allows is its scope, for example "read photos" but not "delete photos".

Two words in that definition need attention. "String" means the token is plain text: a long run of characters. "Authorization" means a permission: who approved what, for which app. So the token is a permission written down as text.

Bearer tokens: the weakness

The door-card comparison has a sharp edge. Whoever holds a bearer token can use it; they don't have to prove they own any key. A copied card opens the door just as well.

The word "bearer" means "the one carrying it". A bearer cheque can be cashed by whoever holds it; the bank does not ask whose it is. A bearer token works the same way. This is simple to build, and it is also the biggest risk in OAuth. Part 11 deals with stolen tokens.

Working: how a token travels with a request

Clients send the token in the Authorization header using the Bearer scheme: Authorization: Bearer followed by the token.

GET /v1/photos?album=holiday HTTP/1.1
Host: photos.example.com
Authorization: Bearer eyJ0eXAiOiJhdCtqd3Qi...

Explanation of the request, line by line.

  1. GET /v1/photos?album=holiday: PrintNest asks for the photos in one album. Nothing secret is in this line.
  2. Host: photos.example.com: which server the request is for.
  3. Authorization: Bearer eyJ...: the header name, then the word Bearer, a space, and the token itself. The API reads this line, checks the token, and only then looks at the album.

Solved example: one token, 40 checks

Given: PrintNest holds one token with scope "read photos". It sends 40 requests for photos and then, by a bug, one request to delete a photo.

Step 1. Each of the 40 read requests carries the same token in the Authorization header.

Step 2. For each one, the API checks that the token is genuine and still valid, and that "read photos" is in its scope. All 40 pass.

Step 3. The delete request carries the same token. The API checks the scope, finds no "delete photos", and refuses.

Result: 41 checks, 40 accepted, 1 refused. You were asked once; the API checked 41 times. That is the division of work: approval once at the login server, checking every time at the API.

Where not to put a token

The spec says bearer tokens should not be passed in page URLs, because URLs end up in places like browser history and logs. Since a bearer token works for whoever holds it, every copy written to a log file is a copy someone else can use.

  • Access token = a permission written as a string; scope = what it allows.
  • A bearer token works for anyone holding it.
  • Send it in the Authorization header, never in the URL.

An access token is a key card with a scope: the API checks the card, not you, and anyone holding the card can use it.

A teammate proposes GET /photos?access_token=eyJ... because it's easier to test. What's the real problem?

  1. URLs are too short for tokens
  2. The API can't read tokens from the query string
  3. The token gets copied into history and logs, where anyone who reads them can use it

A bearer token works for whoever holds it, so every place it gets written down is a place it can be stolen from.

The login dance, step by step

Last part: the key is a bearer token the API checks on every request. Still unsolved: how does the token get from the login server to PrintNest safely?

You log in on the login server's site, in your browser. PrintNest runs on its own servers. How would you get the token from the login server to PrintNest without exposing it?

Split it into two hops. Your browser carries only a short-lived, one-time code back to PrintNest. PrintNest's server then trades that code for the token by calling the login server directly, server to server. The token never passes through the browser.

This two-hop design is the authorization code flow. Every later part adds to this picture, so it is worth going through slowly.

Introduction

Three programs take part, and they are in three different places. Your browser runs on your laptop. PrintNest runs on PrintNest's servers. The login server runs at the photo cloud. Your browser can talk to both servers, and the two servers can talk to each other directly. The flow uses both kinds of path, for different jobs.

Terms used in the flow

Four new terms appear in the diagram. Each is defined here before it is used.

  1. client ID: PrintNest's name at the login server, given when PrintNest registered. It is printed in URLs, so it is not a secret.
  2. redirect URI: the address on PrintNest's site where your browser is sent back after you log in, for example https://photos.example.com/callback. PrintNest registers it in advance.
  3. state: a random value PrintNest makes before sending you away. The login server returns it unchanged. If the value that comes back matches the one PrintNest made, PrintNest knows this callback belongs to the login it started.
  4. authorization code (here, simply "the code"): a short-lived, one-time string the login server sends back through your browser. On its own it gives no access; PrintNest must trade it for a token.

Diagram: the authorization code flow

Explanation of the diagram, step by step

  1. Click Import photos. The flow always starts from the app, because the app is the one that needs access.
  2. Make a secret and its hash. PrintNest creates a random secret and stores it on its own server. It will send only a hash (a one-way scrambled form) of it. Part 5 explains this in full; for now, remember that the secret stays at PrintNest.
  3. Redirect to the login server. PrintNest does not call the login server itself here. It replies to your browser with "go to this address instead". The address carries the client ID, the redirect URI, the scope, the state and the hash.
  4. You log in and approve. This is the only step where your password is typed, and it is typed on the login server's page. The login server also shows you what PrintNest asked for, so you approve with full knowledge.
  5. Redirect back with code and state. The login server now sends your browser to PrintNest's redirect URI, adding the code and the same state value to the address.
  6. Browser delivers code and state. Your browser loads PrintNest's callback page. PrintNest first compares the state with the one it saved in step 3. If they differ, it stops here.
  7. Code plus the secret. PrintNest's server calls the login server directly. The code and the secret travel in this direct call, not through any browser.
  8. Access token. The login server checks the code and the secret and returns the token in the reply to this direct call.
  9. Get photos plus token. PrintNest uses the token as in Part 2.
  10. Photos. The API returns only what the scope allows.

Why the steps are in this order. Steps 3 to 6 go through your browser because only your browser can show you a login page. Steps 7 and 8 avoid your browser because they carry the valuable item, the token. Part 4 explains this split in detail.

The exact parameter names

The parameters have exact names you'll see in every library. The authorization request carries response_type=code and client_id, and usually redirect_uri, scope and state. PKCE adds two parameters to that request: code_challenge and code_challenge_method.

Solved example: the address PrintNest builds in step 3

Given: PrintNest's client ID is photo-app, its redirect URI is https://photos.example.com/callback, it wants the scope photos.read, its state value is Qx7... (shortened here), and the hash of its secret is YMU4z7-lwFN16O0dmZC4Ib7dj3vxWKHvPgrThxB62w4.

https://login.example.com/authorize
  ?response_type=code
  &client_id=photo-app
  &redirect_uri=https%3A%2F%2Fphotos.example.com%2Fcallback
  &scope=photos.read
  &state=Qx7...
  &code_challenge=YMU4z7-lwFN16O0dmZC4Ib7dj3vxWKHvPgrThxB62w4
  &code_challenge_method=S256

Explanation, parameter by parameter.

  • response_type=code asks for a code, not a token, in the redirect back. This one word selects the two-hop design.
  • client_id=photo-app tells the login server which app is asking, so it can show "PrintNest wants to read your photos".
  • redirect_uri says where to send your browser back. It is URL-encoded (: becomes %3A, / becomes %2F) because it sits inside another URL.
  • scope=photos.read is the permission PrintNest asks for. You see it on the approval screen.
  • state is PrintNest's random value, saved in its session, checked in step 6.
  • code_challenge and code_challenge_method=S256 carry the hash of the secret and the way it was made. Part 5 works this hash out by hand.

Result: after you approve, your browser is sent to https://photos.example.com/callback?code=...&state=Qx7.... PrintNest reads state first, then code.

  • Front half (steps 3 to 6) goes through the browser; it carries only a code and the state.
  • Back half (steps 7 and 8) is a direct server call; it carries the secret and the token.
  • The state proves the callback belongs to this login.

The browser carries only a one-time code; the token itself travels server to server.

Put the core of the flow in order.

  1. PrintNest redirects your browser to the login server
  2. You log in and approve
  3. The login server redirects back with a code
  4. PrintNest trades the code (plus secret) for a token, server to server
  5. PrintNest calls the Photos API with the token

Redirect out, approve, redirect back with a code, trade the code on the back channel, then use the token.

Why a code first, and not the token straight away

Last part: the browser carries a code, and the token moves server to server. Still unsolved: why not skip the code?

Every redirect is a URL your browser visits, so everything in it passes through your machine and whatever is watching it. Treat anything in a redirect URL as half-leaked.

Introduction

The flow in Part 3 has an extra hop. It would be simpler for the login server to put the token straight into the redirect. To see why that is not done, we must look at the two kinds of path a message can take.

Definitions: the two channels

So OAuth uses two channels. The front channel is the browser redirect: useful, but leaky. The back channel is a direct server-to-server call. Valuable things go on the back channel.

Places a front-channel URL can end up:

  1. The browser's history, readable by anyone using that laptop.
  2. Browser extensions, which can read the addresses you visit.
  3. Server access logs at the receiving site and any proxy in between.
  4. Another app on a phone that has registered to open the same kind of link.

A back-channel call is an HTTPS request from one server to another. None of the four places above sees it.

Rule: keep the front-channel item worth little

The code on the front channel is built to be worth little:

  • The spec says the code must expire shortly after it is issued and recommends a maximum lifetime of 10 minutes.
  • The client must not use an authorization code more than once. If a code is used more than once, the login server must deny the request.

Short life limits how long a leaked code is dangerous. Single use means that once PrintNest has spent it, a copy is dead.

Solved example: one leaked code, three attackers

Given: the login server issues a code at 10:00:00. PrintNest redeems it at 10:00:02. The code is also written into your browser history.

An attacker finds a code in your browser history 30 minutes after you logged in. Why is it useless to them?

  1. It should have expired (10 minutes is the recommended maximum), and PrintNest already spent it, so a second use is denied
  2. Codes are encrypted
  3. The attacker doesn't know your password

PKCE: making a stolen code worthless

Last part: codes are short-lived and single-use. Still unsolved: what if an attacker grabs the code in the first few seconds and redeems it first?

PKCE was designed against an attack where a malicious app on the same device intercepts the code meant for the real app. The thief can redeem the code well inside the 10-minute window.

Introduction

Part 4 left one gap: an attacker who gets the code and uses it before PrintNest does. Short life and single use do not help, because the attacker is first. We need the login server to ask, at the moment of the trade, "are you the same app that started this login?"

The difficulty is that the start (step 3) went through the browser, where everything is visible. So PrintNest cannot send a secret there. It needs a way to commit to a secret in public without revealing it.

An everyday comparison

Think of a cloakroom ticket torn in half. You keep one half. The coat comes back only to someone holding the matching half.

Where the comparison stops working: anyone who sees the cloakroom's half could forge a matching half. PKCE avoids that by using a hash: seeing the public half does not let you build the private half.

Definitions

That is PKCE (Proof Key for Code Exchange): a method by which the app commits to a random secret when the login starts, and proves it holds that secret when it trades the code.

Two terms come with it. The code verifier is the random secret PrintNest keeps. The code challenge is the hash of the verifier, which is sent in the redirect. A hash here is SHA-256, a function that turns any input into a fixed-size output and cannot be run backwards.

Working

  1. The app makes a code_verifier: a random string of 43 to 128 characters drawn from letters, digits and the symbols - . _ ~.
  2. With the S256 method, the code_challenge is BASE64URL(SHA256(code_verifier)). Only this hash goes in the redirect.
  3. The login server stores the hash alongside the code.
  4. At the token endpoint the server transforms the code_verifier and compares it with the stored challenge; if they differ, it returns an invalid_grant error.

Why each step is needed.

  • Step 1: the verifier must be long and random so nobody can guess it.
  • Step 2: only the hash is sent, because the redirect is public. A hash cannot be turned back into the verifier.
  • Step 3: the server ties the hash to this one code, so the code is only valid together with the matching verifier.
  • Step 4: at the trade, the app reveals the verifier on the back channel, where nobody else sees it. The server repeats the hashing and compares.

Solved example: computing a challenge by hand

Given: PrintNest's verifier is printnest-demo-verifier-0123456789abcdefghi. (A real one is random; this one is readable so you can follow it.)

Step 1. Check the verifier. It has 43 characters, all letters, digits or hyphens. It is valid.

Step 2. Hash it with SHA-256. The output is 32 bytes, written in hexadecimal as 60c538cf…107adb0e.

Step 3. Encode the 32 bytes with base64url. Base64url writes every 3 bytes as 4 characters, using only letters, digits, - and _, so it is safe inside a URL. 32 bytes give 44 characters, the last being a = pad. PKCE drops the pad.

Result: the code challenge is YMU4z7-lwFN16O0dmZC4Ib7dj3vxWKHvPgrThxB62w4 (43 characters). This is the value that appeared in the address in Part 3.

Now the attack. A thief has the code but not the verifier. They send a guess, attacker-guess-0000000000000000000000000000. The login server hashes it and gets rtCvDrjz9YjfXEuR11scJVP10HWyGica368JiOx_rPM. This does not equal the stored YMU4z7…, so the server answers invalid_grant. PrintNest, which sends the real verifier, gets the matching value and receives the token. The page's test suite computes all four values above and checks that this page shows them.

An attacker steals the code and races PrintNest to the token endpoint. They send the code but make up a verifier. What happens?

The login server hashes the made-up verifier. The result doesn't match the stored challenge, so the request fails with invalid_grant. The attacker can't reverse the hash to find the real verifier, and the real one never left PrintNest.

Rules from the current best practice

RFC 9700 notes that PKCE was designed to protect native apps, but its advice now applies to all kinds of clients, including web applications.

  • RFC 9700 says public clients must use PKCE and recommends it for confidential clients too.
  • Clients should use S256, currently the only PKCE method that doesn't expose the verifier in the authorization request.
  • Authorization servers must support PKCE and must reject a token request with a code_verifier when the authorization request carried no code_challenge.

If code_challenge_method is left out, it defaults to plain, where the challenge is the verifier itself. Old code sometimes relies on that. Always send S256.

Wrong belief: "PKCE is only for mobile apps; our web app has a client secret, so it doesn't need it."

Why people hold it: PKCE was first written for apps on phones, which cannot keep a secret.

Why it is wrong: a client secret proves which app is calling, not which login the code came from. An attacker who injects a stolen code into the real web app's callback gets the real app to redeem it. PKCE ties each code to the one browser session that started it. That is why the current best practice recommends it for every kind of client.

  • Verifier: long, random, and never leaves the app until the back-channel trade.
  • Challenge = base64url(SHA-256(verifier)), sent in the redirect.
  • Wrong verifier at the trade = invalid_grant.
  • Always S256, never plain.

PKCE binds the code to a secret only the real app holds, so a stolen code can't be redeemed.

Explain PKCE to a teammate who thinks "the code is short-lived, so we don't need it".

Short-lived isn't enough, because an attacker who intercepts the redirect can redeem the code within seconds. With PKCE the app creates a random verifier, keeps it, and sends only its SHA-256 hash in the redirect. To trade the code for a token, the app must present the original verifier. The server hashes it and compares. A thief who has the code but not the verifier fails, and the verifier never travelled through the browser.

Short-lived tokens, and how to renew them

Last part: PKCE makes a stolen code worthless. Still unsolved: a stolen access token still works until it expires. How long should that be?

Make the access token last a year and a stolen one works for a year. Make it expire after ten minutes and you are sent to the login page again every ten minutes. Neither is acceptable.

Introduction

Two needs pull in opposite directions. Security wants a short life, so a stolen token dies quickly. Convenience wants a long life, so you are not asked to log in again and again. One token cannot satisfy both. The answer is to use two tokens with two different jobs.

Definition

The fix is a second, longer-lived key that is used in only one place. That is a refresh token.

  • Refresh tokens are used only with the authorization server and are never sent to resource servers.
  • The client exchanges a refresh token at the token endpoint with grant_type=refresh_token, and the server may issue a new refresh token at the same time.

So the access token is shown everywhere but lives briefly; the refresh token lives long but is shown to one server only. A thief who reads API logs finds only access tokens, which die soon.

Working

  1. At login, PrintNest receives an access token and a refresh token.
  2. PrintNest calls the API with the access token until it expires.
  3. PrintNest sends the refresh token to the login server, on the back channel.
  4. The login server returns a fresh access token (and possibly a new refresh token).
  5. PrintNest carries on. You are not asked to log in.

Why each step is needed. Step 2 keeps the frequently used token cheap to lose. Step 3 goes to the login server only, so the long-lived token is exposed to the fewest places. Step 4 gives the login server a regular chance to say no: if you cancelled PrintNest, the next refresh fails and access stops.

Two kinds of client

How much to trust the app depends on where it runs. The spec splits clients into confidential clients, which can keep a secret (typically server-side apps), and public clients, which can't (native apps and apps running in the browser).

Why a phone app cannot keep a secret: the app file is installed on millions of phones. Anyone can unpack it and read every string inside. A secret shipped to everyone is not a secret.

A refresh token sitting on a phone is a juicy target, so public clients get an extra rule:

  • For public clients, RFC 9700 requires refresh tokens to be sender-constrained or rotated. Sender-constrained means the token works only together with proof of a key the app holds.
  • Rotation means every refresh returns a new refresh token and invalidates the previous one. If an old refresh token is used again, which happens when both a thief and the real app hold it, the login server can detect the reuse and revoke the active refresh token.

Solved example: rotation catches a thief

Given: PrintNest's phone app holds refresh token R1. Malware copies R1 at 09:00. Rotation is on.

TimeWhoSendsLogin server does
09:10Real appR1Valid. Returns a new access token and R2. Marks R1 as used.
09:15ThiefR1R1 was already used. Reuse detected. Revokes R2 as well.
09:20Real appR2R2 is revoked. Refuses. The app asks you to log in again.

Result: the thief got nothing. The real user had to log in once more, which is a small cost. The login server can also record the reuse as a security event. If the thief had used R1 first, the order would be reversed, but the outcome is the same: the second use of R1 triggers the revocation.

  • Access token: short life, sent to the API.
  • Refresh token: long life, sent only to the login server.
  • Public clients (phone and browser apps) cannot keep secrets; their refresh tokens must be rotated or sender-constrained.

Access tokens stay short-lived; a refresh token, shown only to the login server, renews them without a new login.

The PrintNest phone app stores a refresh token on the device. What does RFC 9700 require for it?

  1. Encrypt it with PrintNest's client secret
  2. Bind it to a key the app holds (sender-constrained), or rotate it on every use
  3. Never give phone apps refresh tokens

A public client can't keep a secret, so the refresh token itself must be either unusable elsewhere or single-use.

The client fits in about 50 lines. Most real bugs come from three shortcuts: skipping the state check, sending PKCE as plain, and keeping the verifier somewhere the browser can see.

Introduction

We now write PrintNest's side of Part 3 as a program. It has four functions. challenge_for does the hashing from Part 5. make_pkce makes a verifier and its challenge. start_login builds the address for step 3. on_callback handles steps 6 to 8. The program uses only Python's standard library, so every line is visible.

Program

Explanation of the program

  1. Constants. AUTHZ_ENDPOINT is where your browser is sent to log in. TOKEN_ENDPOINT is where the back-channel trade happens. CLIENT_ID and REDIRECT_URI are the values PrintNest registered.
  2. challenge_for(verifier) does exactly the three steps of the solved example in Part 5: SHA-256 gives 32 bytes, base64url encodes them, and .rstrip(b"=") removes the padding.
  3. make_pkce() calls secrets.token_urlsafe(64): 64 random bytes, encoded as 86 URL-safe characters. That is inside the allowed 43 to 128 and uses only allowed characters. The secrets module is used, not random, because random is predictable and must not be used for security values.
  4. start_login(session) makes a fresh state (32 random bytes, 43 characters) and a fresh PKCE pair for every login. It saves the state and the verifier in session. In a real web app this is a server-side session found through a cookie, so neither value is ever placed in a URL. Then it builds the address with urlencode, which also does the %3A and %2F encoding.
  5. on_callback(session, params) first compares the returned state with the saved one using secrets.compare_digest, a comparison whose timing does not reveal how many characters matched. session.pop removes the state so it cannot be used twice. On a mismatch it raises an error before any network call.
  6. The token request. It sends a form body to the token endpoint with the five fields described below, and returns the JSON reply.

The token request sends grant_type=authorization_code and the code, plus the redirect_uri if the first request had one, and with PKCE the code_verifier; a client that doesn't authenticate also sends its client_id. A server-side PrintNest would authenticate itself here, because it is a confidential client.

Wrong belief: "We use PKCE, so the order of checks in the callback does not matter."

Why it is wrong: the callback should reject a bad state before trading the code. Trading first means doing work and possibly getting a token for a login this browser never started. Checking state first fails closed and costs nothing.

  • New state and new S256 verifier for every login.
  • Both kept server-side and deleted after one use.
  • Check state first; trade the code once.

A correct client makes a fresh state and S256 verifier per login, keeps both server-side, checks state first, then redeems the code once.

  3. Reject it without calling the token endpoint, and restart the login

A state mismatch means this browser didn't start this login. Fail closed: stop before trading the code, even if you also use PKCE.

Build it: the API checks every token

Last part: the client gets a token correctly. Still unsolved: what exactly must the Photos API check before trusting it?

The Photos API takes 2,000 requests a second, each with a token. Skip one check and a token meant for another service, or an expired one, gets in.

Introduction

The API is the last line of defence. Everything before it only matters if the API refuses tokens it should refuse. In this part we list the checks, see a real token taken apart, and walk through the code that performs the checks.

Definition

When the login server issues access tokens as signed JWTs, the API can check them locally. A JWT has three parts separated by dots: a header (how it was signed), a payload (the facts, called claims), and a signature. The login server signs with its private key; the API checks the signature with the matching public key, so the API never needs to call the login server for each request.

RFC 9068 says a JWT access token must declare itself in its typ header, normally as at+jwt, which lets APIs tell it apart from other JWTs such as ID tokens.

The checks

  • The resource server must check that typ is at+jwt, that iss exactly matches the expected issuer, that aud contains its own identifier, that the signature is valid, and that exp is in the future, and must reject tokens with alg none.
  • Required claims in a JWT access token are iss, exp, aud, sub, client_id, iat and jti.
  • If scopes were requested, the token should carry them in a scope claim.

In plain words: iss is who issued it, aud is who it is meant for, exp is when it expires, sub is the user, client_id is the app, iat is when it was issued, and jti is a unique id for this one token.

Solved example: checking one token by hand

Given: the Photos API's own identifier is https://photos.example.com, it trusts the issuer https://login.example.com, the current time is 1791763500 (a Unix timestamp), and the request asks to read photos. The token, decoded, is:

{"alg": "RS256", "typ": "at+jwt"}
{"iss": "https://login.example.com",
 "aud": "https://photos.example.com",
 "sub": "u1", "client_id": "photo-app",
 "iat": 1791763200, "exp": 1791763800,
 "jti": "j1", "scope": "photos.read"}

Program

Explanation of the program

  1. jwt.get_unverified_header(token) reads the header without trusting it, only to check typ. An ID token or any other JWT is turned away here.
  2. jwt.decode(...) does the main work: it verifies the signature with public_key, then checks exp, aud against AUDIENCE and iss against ISSUER. If any check fails it raises an error.
  3. algorithms=["RS256"] is a fixed list written in our code. The token's own alg header is never used to choose. This is also what rejects alg: none.
  4. options={"require": [...]} refuses tokens that are missing any of the required claims, instead of silently treating a missing exp as "never expires".
  5. require_scope is a separate step because scope depends on the request: reading needs photos.read, deleting would need something else. A missing scope raises insufficient_scope.

Run against test tokens, it accepted the valid one and rejected each of these: wrong audience, wrong issuer, expired, an ID token, alg: none, a token signed by another key, and a token missing the needed scope.

When a token is rejected, the error invalid_token goes with HTTP 401 and insufficient_scope with HTTP 403. The difference matters to the client: 401 means "get a new token", 403 means "this token is fine but not allowed to do this".

PyJWT's documentation warns never to build the algorithms list from the token's own alg header. If you trust the token to say how it was signed, an attacker picks the algorithm.

Wrong belief: "The signature is valid, so the token is good."

Why people hold it: the signature is the cryptographic part, so it feels like the important check.

Why it is wrong: a valid signature proves only that our login server issued the token. The same login server also issues tokens for the billing API, ID tokens, and tokens that expired last week. Audience, type and expiry decide whether the token is valid for this API, now.

"Your API accepts tokens from one login server. What exactly do you validate, and in what order?" A strong answer lists type, signature with a fixed algorithm, issuer, audience, expiry, then scope per endpoint, and explains that the first five decide 401 and the last decides 403. Bonus: mention that keys are fetched and cached, not hard-coded, so key rotation does not need a deploy.

  • Checks: typ, signature (fixed algorithm list), iss, aud, exp, then scope.
  • Bad or wrong-audience token: 401 invalid_token. Missing scope: 403 insufficient_scope.
  • Never let the token choose its own algorithm.

An API trusts a token only after checking type, signature with a fixed algorithm, issuer, audience, expiry and scope.

A token arrives with a valid signature from your login server, but its aud is the billing API. What should the Photos API do?

  1. Accept it: the signature is valid
  2. Reject it with 401 invalid_token
  3. Reject it with 403 insufficient_scope

The audience check stops a token issued for one service from being replayed at another.

Pick the right flow for the job

Last part: the API checks every token. Still unsolved: the code flow assumes a person with a browser. What about jobs, TVs and CLIs?

A nightly billing job has no user. A smart TV has no keyboard worth typing on. Each needs a different way to get a token.

Introduction

OAuth calls each way of getting a token a grant (also called a flow). The code flow of Parts 3 to 5 is one grant. The right grant depends on one question: who is present when the token is needed, and what can they type on?

Explanation of each row.

  2. No person. A nightly job acts for itself, not for a user. Nobody can approve anything, so the app proves its own identity (its client credentials) directly to the login server and receives a token. Because this needs a secret the app can keep, only server-side apps may use it.

  4. Native apps. Same as row 1, but the login page must open in the phone's real browser. An embedded web view inside the app would let the app read what you type, which defeats Part 1.

Working of the device flow

The device flow moves the login to a second screen:

Explanation of the diagram.

  1. The TV asks the login server to begin. It sends only its client ID; it has no user yet.
  2. The login server replies with two codes. The device code is long and secret; the TV keeps it. The user code is short, so a person can read it off the screen and type it. The reply also has the web address to visit.

Given: a command-line tool on a server you reached over SSH. There is no browser on that server.

Wrong belief: "Client credentials is the simple version of the code flow, so we can use it for our users too."

Why it is wrong: client credentials gives a token for the app itself. There is no user in it, so the API cannot tell which user's data is allowed. Use it only when the app acts on its own behalf.

A strong answer picks the device flow (or code + PKCE with a localhost redirect if the CLI runs where a browser exists), explains why client credentials is wrong (no user), and mentions storing the refresh token safely on disk.

  • Person + browser: code + PKCE. No person: client credentials. No keyboard: device flow.
  • Native apps open the login in the system browser.
  • The password grant must not be used.

A nightly job reconciles invoices by calling your ledger API. No person is involved. Which flow?

  1. Client credentials
  2. Device authorization

Explain what "doing OAuth correctly" means on the client side, on the API side, and when choosing a flow.

The client creates a fresh random state and an S256 PKCE pair for every login, keeps both in a server-side session, rejects callbacks with the wrong state, and redeems each code once. The API checks the token type, verifies the signature with a fixed algorithm list, and checks issuer, audience, expiry and scope before serving anything. Attacks on the redirect

Last part: pick the flow by who is present. Still unsolved: the redirect runs through a browser an attacker can steer. Where does it break?

An attacker can't touch PrintNest's servers, but they can send you a link. Every attack below starts with your browser visiting a URL the attacker built.

Introduction

Part 4 said the front channel is leaky. Here we study how an attacker uses that leak. There are four classic attacks. For each one we give the idea, a worked case, and the rule that blocks it.

Types of redirect attack

  1. Loose redirect matching. If the login server accepts "anything starting with" the registered URL, the attacker picks a redirect that lands on their site, and the code goes to them. Authorization servers must compare redirect URIs by exact string match; the only exception is the port of a localhost redirect for native apps.
  2. Open redirectors. Clients and authorization servers must not expose open redirectors, pages that forward the browser to any URL given in a query parameter. An exact-match redirect that then bounces anywhere is no better than a loose one.
  3. Forged callbacks (CSRF). The attacker makes your browser deliver their code to PrintNest, so you end up using the attacker's account. Clients must prevent CSRF, either with a one-time state value bound to the user's browser or by relying on PKCE when the authorization server is known to support it.
  4. Mix-up. An app that trusts several login servers can be tricked into sending a code to the wrong one. Such a client should use the iss response parameter to tell the servers apart, or failing that, a distinct redirect URI per server.

A login server accepts any redirect URI that starts with https://printnest.com. What URL would an attacker register or use?

Something like https://printnest.com.attacker.io/cb. It starts with the allowed prefix but is a different domain the attacker owns. The code lands on their server. Exact string matching closes this attack.

Solved example: a forged callback

Given: PrintNest does not check state. The attacker logs in to PrintNest with their own photo-cloud account and stops just before the callback, keeping their code ATK-CODE.

Step 1. The attacker sends you a link to https://photos.example.com/callback?code=ATK-CODE.

Step 2. You click it while logged in to PrintNest. Your browser delivers the attacker's code to PrintNest.

Step 3. PrintNest trades the code and links the resulting token to your PrintNest account.

Result: your PrintNest account is now connected to the attacker's photo cloud. Photos you upload to "your" cloud through PrintNest go to the attacker. With a state check, step 3 never happens: the callback has no state, or one your browser never made, so PrintNest rejects it.

A tempting shortcut: allow any subdomain so preview deployments like https://pr-123.printnest.dev/callback work. Then any subdomain an attacker can take over becomes a place to receive codes. Register each redirect URI explicitly instead.

"PKCE is on. Do we still need the state parameter?" A strong answer: PKCE also stops injected codes, and RFC 9700 allows a client to rely on PKCE for CSRF protection once it has ensured the login server supports PKCE. Keep state anyway if you can't guarantee that support, or if you need it to carry where to send the user after login. Show you know both jobs.

Wrong belief: "The redirect stays on our own domain, so it is safe."

Why it is wrong: staying on your domain for one hop is not enough. If any page on that domain forwards the browser elsewhere, the code travels on with it. Safety needs both exact matching and no open redirectors.

  • Exact redirect matching; no open redirectors anywhere.
  • State (or verified PKCE support) on every login.
  • Several login servers: check iss.

The redirect is only as safe as its matching: exact URIs, no open redirectors, and state or PKCE on every login.

A login server accepts any redirect URI under https://photos.example.com/. PrintNest also has a page /go?next= that forwards the browser anywhere. What can an attacker do?

  1. Nothing: the code never leaves PrintNest's domain
  2. Redeem the code without PKCE
  3. Set the redirect URI to /go?next= their site, so the code is forwarded to them

Loose matching plus an open redirector hands the code to the attacker. That's why both rules exist: exact matching, and no open redirectors anywhere.

When a token is stolen

Last part: attacks on the redirect. Still unsolved: attackers who skip the flow and steal tokens that were already issued.

In April 2022, attackers used OAuth user tokens stolen from two GitHub integrators, Heroku and Travis CI, to list and clone private repositories of dozens of organisations. GitHub spotted it when a compromised AWS key was used to access npm's production infrastructure; it revoked the tokens tied to its own use of the affected apps and asked Heroku and Travis CI to revoke all the others.

No flow was broken there. The tokens were valid, just held by the wrong people. Bearer tokens can't tell the difference, so defence is about limiting what a stolen token can do and for how long.

Introduction

All the earlier parts protect the path by which a token is issued. This part assumes that has already failed somewhere: a token was copied out of a database, a log file or a laptop. The question becomes: how much damage can that copy do, and how do we stop it?

Damage has three dimensions: what the token allows (scope and audience), how long it works (lifetime), and who can use it (anyone, or only the real app).

Methods of defence

  1. Keep them narrow. RFC 9700 says access tokens should be audience-restricted to a specific resource server and limited to the privileges needed.
  2. Keep them short. A stolen token that expires in minutes is a small problem. Pair short tokens with refresh tokens.
  3. Bind them to the sender. RFC 9700 says authorization and resource servers should use sender-constrained access tokens, through mutual TLS (RFC 8705) or DPoP (RFC 9449).
  4. Be able to kill them. The revocation endpoint takes a token by POST, and revoking a refresh token should also invalidate the access tokens issued from the same grant, if the server supports that.

How each method reduces damage. Method 1 reduces what. Method 2 reduces how long. Method 3 changes who: from anyone to the key holder only. Method 4 lets you end how long early, once you know about the theft.

Solved example: lifetime decides the exposure

Given: a token is written to a log file at 12:00. An attacker reads the log at 12:30.

Token lifetimeStill valid at 12:30?Attacker's window
One hourYes12:30 to 13:00
Five minutesNo, expired at 12:05None
Five minutes, but sender-constrainedIrrelevantNone: no private key, so it cannot be used at any time

Sender-constrained tokens with DPoP

DPoP turns a bearer token into one that only works with its owner's private key. With DPoP the client signs a small proof JWT for each request with its private key; the proof names the HTTP method and URL, includes a hash of the access token when calling an API, and travels in a DPoP header.

Because the proof names the method and URL, a copied proof is useless for any other request. Because it is signed with the private key, an attacker cannot make a fresh one.

What the user sees, how to detect it

Usually the user sees nothing: the attacker reads data quietly with a valid token. That is why detection must come from the server side. Useful signals: one client ID suddenly reading far more data than usual; the same token used from two very different networks; refresh-token reuse events from Part 6; and, as in the GitHub case, a credential found in that data being used somewhere it should not be.

"A partner tells you your OAuth tokens leaked from their logs. Walk me through the next hour." A strong answer: revoke the partner's refresh tokens and grants, so access tokens die at expiry; for high-risk APIs, block the partner's client ID or switch those APIs to introspection so revocation bites immediately; pull API logs by client ID for unusual reads; tell affected users. Then the fix: shorter lifetimes, audience-restricted tokens, and sender-constrained tokens for that partner.

Wrong belief: "We use HTTPS everywhere, so our tokens cannot be stolen."

Why it is wrong: HTTPS protects a token while it travels. It does nothing once the token is stored in a database, a log file or a backup. In the GitHub case the tokens were stolen from the two integrators themselves; how they travelled over the network made no difference.

  • Limit what (scope, audience), how long (short life) and who (sender-constrained).
  • Keep the ability to revoke.
  • Theft is invisible to the user; detect it in server logs.

A stolen bearer token works until it expires, so make tokens narrow, short-lived, revocable, and ideally bound to a key.

An attacker copies a DPoP-bound access token from a log file. Why can't they use it?

  1. The token is encrypted
  2. Every request needs a fresh proof signed with the client's private key, which the attacker doesn't have
  3. The API only accepts it from the client's IP address

The token is bound to a key pair. Without the private key, the attacker can't produce the per-request proof.

What breaks first at 10× traffic

Last part: limit what a stolen token can do. Still unsolved: checking tokens costs something on every request. What happens when traffic grows?

The Photos API goes from 200 to 2,000 requests a second. Every one of them carries a token that must be checked. There are two ways to check.

Introduction

In Part 8 the API checked a JWT using only a public key. That is one method. The other is to ask the login server about each token. Both are correct; they differ in cost and in how quickly a cancelled token stops working. At low traffic either is fine. At ten times the traffic, the difference decides what fails first.

Definitions

  1. Token introspection: the API sends the token to the login server and asks "is this active, and what does it allow?"
  2. Local JWT check: the API checks the signature and claims itself, as in Part 8, using the login server's public keys kept in memory.

Explanation of the diagram. In step 1 every incoming request causes an arrow from the API to the login server and a reply back. The login server sees every request and therefore always knows if a token was cancelled. In step 2 the arrow from the API goes to a key cache inside the API itself; only the rare dashed arrow reaches the login server, to fetch keys. The login server no longer sees each request, so it cannot tell the API that a token was cancelled.

  • With token introspection the API POSTs the token to the authorization server, which answers with JSON whose required active field says whether the token is currently valid.
  • RFC 7662 describes the caching trade-off: a longer cache cuts load on the introspection endpoint, but a revoked token can stay usable until the cache entry expires.
  • For local checks, the authorization server can publish its signing keys at a jwks_uri listed in its metadata, and RFC 9068 says it should do so for resource servers. By default that metadata lives at /.well-known/oauth-authorization-server on the issuer's host, placed before any path the issuer URL has.

Solved example: sizing the introspection load

Given: 2,000 requests a second from 3,000 active tokens, spread evenly, and an introspection call that takes a few milliseconds.

Case 1, no cache. Every request needs a call: 2,000 calls a second reach the login server, and every API request waits for one of them.

Case 2, cache each answer for half a minute. Each token is introspected at most once per half minute. 3,000 tokens ÷ 30 = at most 100 calls a second, a 20-fold reduction. The price: a cancelled token can keep working for up to half a minute.

Case 3, local JWT checks. Zero calls per request. Keys are fetched occasionally. The price: a cancelled token keeps working until its own expiry.

Result: the cache length is a dial between load and revocation delay. Choosing it is a design decision you should be able to state with numbers.

Comparison at 10× load

What the user sees. With introspection under strain, every photo loads slowly, and if the endpoint fails, every request fails: the login server has become a single point of failure for the API. With local checks, a key-rotation problem shows up as users suddenly being logged out (401 on valid-looking tokens) right after the login server switched keys.

PyJWT's decode function takes a leeway argument that allows a small margin when checking expiry, for clocks that are slightly out of sync.

"Your API validates JWTs locally. How do you make revocation fast enough?" A strong answer names the trade-off: local checks can't see revocation, so keep access tokens short (minutes) and revoke refresh tokens; for the few endpoints where seconds matter (payouts, admin), use introspection with a short cache. You pay latency only where the risk justifies it.

Wrong belief: "Revoking a token at the login server stops it everywhere, immediately."

Why it is wrong: it stops it only where the API asks the login server. An API that checks JWTs locally never asks, so it keeps accepting the token until expiry. Revocation speed is a property of how the API checks, not of the revoke call.

  • Introspection: fresh answers, one call per request (or per cache window).
  • Local JWT: no calls, but revocation waits for expiry.
  • Failure signals: introspection p99 and errors; 401 spikes after key changes.

Introspection buys instant revocation with a network call per request; local JWT checks buy speed and give up revocation until expiry, so most systems mix the two.

Your API is growing 10×. Explain which token-checking approach you'd use and what breaks first with each.

Introspection asks the login server about every token, so at 10× the introspection endpoint takes 10× the calls and its latency is added to every request; caching helps but delays revocation. Local JWT checks scale with the API because they only need cached public keys, but a revoked token keeps working until it expires, and key rotation or clock skew can cause sudden 401s. I'd use short-lived JWTs checked locally for most endpoints and introspection with a short cache for the few high-risk ones.

OAuth is not login: where OpenID Connect fits

Last part: scale and revocation trade-offs. Still unsolved: PrintNest wants to show "Hi, Asha". Can it use the access token for that?

An access token says what PrintNest may do. It doesn't reliably say who is sitting at the keyboard, or that they logged in just now.

Introduction

Two questions are often confused. Authorization: what may this app do? Authentication: who is this user, and did they just prove it? OAuth answers the first. An app that uses an access token to answer the second is reading a document addressed to someone else: the access token is written for the API, and its format and contents are the API's business.

Definition

OpenID Connect is an identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0; OAuth by itself does not tell the app who the user is or how they authenticated.

  • An OpenID Connect request must include openid in its scope.
  • OpenID Connect adds the ID token, a JWT whose required claims include iss, sub, aud, exp and iat, with aud including the client's ID. The ID token is for the app; the access token is for the API.

Where tokens live in a single-page app

For single-page apps the question is where tokens live. RFC 10017 (August 2026), on browser-based apps, strongly recommends the Backend for Frontend pattern for business applications, sensitive applications and apps that handle personal data. In a BFF a server-side component acts as the confidential OAuth client and keeps the tokens, so there are no tokens in the browser to steal. RFC 10017 notes one attack remains: someone who hijacks the app inside the user's browser can still act through it, a problem inherent to web applications.

Working of a BFF, in short. (1) The browser talks only to the BFF, using an ordinary session cookie. (2) The BFF runs the code flow of Part 3 as a confidential client. (3) The BFF stores the tokens on the server. (4) When the browser needs photos, it asks the BFF, which attaches the access token and calls the API. A script injected into the page can still make requests through the BFF while the page is open, but it cannot carry the tokens away for later.

RFC 6749 is still the core OAuth 2.0 spec; RFC 8252, RFC 8996 and RFC 9700 update it. OAuth 2.1, which folds these best practices into one document meant to replace RFC 6749 and RFC 6750, is still an IETF working-group draft as of September 2026. The rules on this page are those of the current best-practice document.

Wrong belief: "OpenID Connect replaces OAuth."

Why it is wrong: it is built on top of OAuth. An OpenID Connect login is an OAuth code flow with openid in the scope, which returns an ID token as well. Everything in Parts 3 to 12 still applies.

  • OAuth: what may the app do. OpenID Connect: who is the user.
  • ID token is read by the app; access token is read by the API.
  • Single-page apps with personal data: keep tokens in a BFF.

OAuth answers "what may this app do"; OpenID Connect answers "who is this user". Use each for its own question.

PrintNest's new single-page app must show "Hi, Asha" and call the Photos API. Which design fits current guidance?

  1. OpenID Connect through a BFF: the ID token identifies Asha, the backend keeps the access token and calls the API
  2. Decode the access token in the browser to get her name
  3. Use the implicit grant to get a token faster

Identity comes from the ID token; API access comes from the access token; and the BFF keeps tokens out of the browser.

Interview drill: three designs

Last part: OAuth for access, OpenID Connect for identity. Now use all of it the way an interviewer will ask.

Expect OAuth to come up inside a design, not as a definition. For each prompt, write your answer before revealing.

How to answer a design question on OAuth

A good answer follows the same order every time, so you do not forget a part under pressure:

  1. Who is present? A user with a browser, a machine, or a device without a keyboard. This picks the grant (Part 9).
  2. What kind of client? Confidential or public. This decides PKCE and refresh-token rules (Parts 5 and 6).
  3. How does the API check tokens? Locally, by introspection, or both (Parts 8 and 12).
  4. How fast must revocation work? This sets token lifetimes and cache lengths (Part 12).
  5. What if a token is stolen? Narrow, short, sender-constrained (Part 11).
  6. State the trade-off you are paying for, in one sentence.

Design 1. You're building a mobile app for a lending startup. It calls your own APIs for balances and repayments. How do users sign in and how does the app call the APIs?

Authorization code + PKCE (S256) in the system browser, with OpenID Connect for sign-in. Access tokens short-lived and audience-restricted to the lending API. Refresh tokens rotated on every use, because a phone app is a public client. The API validates JWTs locally; repayment endpoints use introspection with a short cache so a revoked session stops within seconds.

Trade-off to say out loud: introspection on repayments adds a network call; you accept that latency for money-moving endpoints only.

Design 2. Forty internal microservices call each other. Security wants each call authenticated and limited. What do you do?

Client credentials, one client per service. RFC 9700 recommends asymmetric client authentication, such as mutual TLS or signed JWTs with a private key, over shared secrets. Tokens audience-restricted per target service with narrow scopes, validated locally with a cached key set.

Trade-off to say out loud: a login server on the hot path of service start-up is a dependency; cache tokens until shortly before expiry and alert on token-endpoint errors.

Design 3. When an employee leaves, their access to every internal tool must stop within one minute. Tools use OAuth. How?

Revoke the employee's refresh tokens and grants at the login server, which may also kill the access tokens from those grants if the server supports it. Access tokens validated locally must then expire within the minute, so keep them very short, or have the sensitive tools introspect with a cache under a minute.

Trade-off to say out loud: one-minute revocation costs either very frequent refreshes or introspection traffic. Say which tools deserve it.

Wrong belief: "A strong interview answer names the most secure option everywhere."

Why it is wrong: every protection has a cost: introspection costs latency, very short tokens cost refresh traffic, sender-constraining costs key management. Interviewers at senior level look for the reason you spend that cost on some endpoints and not on others.

  • Answer order: who is present, client type, token checking, revocation speed, theft, trade-off.
  • Always say what you are giving up.

In a design, OAuth decisions are trade-offs between revocation speed, latency and blast radius; say which one you're paying for and why.

Final teach-back: explain OAuth end to end, as you would at the start of a design interview. The problem, how it works, how you'd build it, what breaks, and when you'd pick something else.

Apps need to act on a user's data without holding the user's password, with access that's limited and revocable. In the code flow the user logs in at the login server; the browser carries back only a short, single-use code; the app trades it for a token on a direct server call, with PKCE proving it's the same app that started. The client keeps state and the verifier server-side; the API checks the token's type, signature with a fixed algorithm, issuer, audience, expiry and scope. It breaks at the redirect (loose matching, open redirectors, CSRF), through stolen bearer tokens, and at scale through introspection load or slow revocation. Use code with PKCE for users, client credentials for services, the device flow for TVs and CLIs, OpenID Connect when you need identity, and a BFF to keep tokens out of single-page apps.
